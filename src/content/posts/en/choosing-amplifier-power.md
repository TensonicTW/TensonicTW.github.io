## The Problem

When shopping for a karaoke amplifier for a private room or banquet venue, buyers often compare rated-power numbers directly — bigger looks like better value. In practice this backfires two ways after installation: oversized power wastes budget and stresses the speakers, or undersized power clips and distorts at full load. Rated power should be derived from the space's actual conditions, not chosen by comparing numbers.

## Analysis: What Should Rated Power Be Sized Against

Three main variables determine the rated-power requirement:

1. **Floor area and ceiling height.** For a typical small-to-medium private room or banquet space (roughly 50–100 m²) with ordinary vocal and music playback needs, a 200–300W class amplifier is usually sufficient.
2. **Speaker count and zoning.** If one amplifier needs to drive both a main front-of-room speaker set and a background speaker set (e.g. the main listening point plus corridor background music), what's needed isn't simply more total wattage — it's whether the amplifier has independent speaker output groups so power can be allocated by zone, instead of overloading a single output.
3. **Dynamic range of the use case.** Karaoke involves vocals, backing music, and occasional loud peaks; the instantaneous dynamic demand exceeds the average playback level. This is exactly why rated power should carry headroom rather than being sized to the average requirement.

## The S-6000's Power Layout as a Reference

Tensonic's S-6000 all-digital KTV integrated amplifier is rated at 300W. Despite a chassis only 25cm deep, it provides 4 independent speaker output terminals (front and rear zones configurable separately) — meaning that 300W isn't concentrated on a single speaker group, but can be allocated across a main listening zone and a background zone according to the room's layout. This power-allocation flexibility reflects real-world performance better than the total wattage figure alone.

The S-6000 also includes automatic feedback suppression, which connects indirectly to power sizing: when microphone feedback (howling) isn't handled well, the common instinctive reaction is to "turn up the power to overpower it" — but that's the wrong direction. The correct approach is to suppress the feedback at its source through signal processing, not to force it down with raw power, which only accelerates wear on the speakers and amplifier.

## Method

A recommended sizing sequence:

1. Confirm floor area, planned speaker count, and zone layout first.
2. Convert that into a baseline power requirement, then add a 20–30% dynamic margin as the floor for rated power.
3. Confirm whether the amplifier has multiple independent outputs, so power can be allocated by actual zone rather than one speaker group carrying the entire load.
4. If the venue has a history of feedback problems, prioritise a model with digital feedback suppression rather than simply sizing up the power.

## Follow-Up Recommendations

At the planning stage, measure the actual floor area and planned speaker positions on site rather than estimating from floor-area figures alone. If the venue's layout may change later — rooms combined, an outdoor karaoke zone added — favour a model with multiple independent outputs to preserve expansion flexibility.
